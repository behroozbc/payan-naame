\section{Fine-Tuning}

Fine-tuning is a transfer learning technique in which a pre-trained model is adapted to a downstream task by updating its parameters on a task-specific dataset. While pre-trained large language models (LLMs) acquire broad linguistic and factual knowledge during the pre-training phase, they often require additional adaptation to perform effectively in specialised domains or to follow specific instruction formats \cite{radford2018improving}. Fine-tuning allows the model to specialise without the computational expense of training from scratch.

Full fine-tuning, wherein all parameters of the model are updated, remains the most direct approach. However, as model sizes have grown to hundreds of billions of parameters, full fine-tuning has become prohibitively expensive in terms of both memory and computation. For a model with \(P\) parameters, full fine-tuning requires storing and updating gradients for all \(P\) parameters, necessitating multiple GPUs even for modestly sized models. This scalability challenge has motivated the development of parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods, which update only a small subset of the model's parameters while keeping the majority frozen.

\subsection{LoRA}

Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) is a parameter-efficient fine-tuning method that addresses the computational limitations of full fine-tuning \cite{hu2022lora}. The key insight underlying LoRA is that the weight updates incurred during fine-tuning exhibit a low intrinsic rank; that is, the change \(\Delta \mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}\) to a pre-trained weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}_0\) can be decomposed into a product of two low-rank matrices:

\[
    \mathbf{W}_0 + \Delta \mathbf{W} = \mathbf{W}_0 + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{A},
\]

where \(\mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}\), \(\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}\), and the rank \(r \ll \min(d, k)\). During fine-tuning, \(\mathbf{W}_0\) remains frozen, and only the matrices \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) are updated. This decomposition drastically reduces the number of trainable parameters. For example, with \(d = k = 4096\) and \(r = 8\), the number of trainable parameters is reduced from approximately \(16.8 \times 10^6\) to approximately \(65 \times 10^3\), representing a reduction of over two orders of magnitude.

In practice, LoRA is applied to the attention projection matrices of the Transformer architecture---namely \(\mathbf{W}_q\), \(\mathbf{W}_k\), \(\mathbf{W}_v\), and \(\mathbf{W}_o\)---while the parameters of the feed-forward layers and the embedding matrices remain frozen. The scaling factor \(\alpha / r\) controls the magnitude of the adaptation, where \(\alpha\) is a hyperparameter typically set to the first value of \(r\) used during experimentation.

Empirically, LoRA achieves performance comparable to full fine-tuning on a range of natural language processing benchmarks, including question answering and text classification, while reducing the memory footprint to a level that permits fine-tuning on a single consumer-grade GPU \cite{hu2022lora}. This efficiency has made LoRA the de facto standard for adapting LLMs to domain-specific tasks, including the educational retrieval and generation tasks explored in this thesis.

\subsection{QLoRA}

Quantized Low-Rank Adaptation (QLoRA) extends LoRA by combining low-rank adaptation with model quantisation, further reducing the memory requirements for fine-tuning large language models \cite{dettmers2023qlora}. The central innovation of QLoRA is the use of the \emph{normalFloat4} (NF4) data type, a information-theoretically optimal quantisation scheme for normally distributed weights. By quantising the frozen pre-trained weights \(\mathbf{W}_0\) to 4-bit precision, QLoRA reduces the memory required to store the base model by approximately a factor of four relative to the standard 16-bit half-precision representation.

QLoRA employs a double quantisation strategy: the quantisation constants themselves are further quantised to 8-bit, yielding additional memory savings. During the forward and backward passes, the 4-bit weights are dequantised on the fly to the computation data type (typically bfloat16 or float16), and the LoRA adaptor matrices \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) are maintained in full precision. This design ensures that the computational graph remains differentiable while the memory footprint of the base model is minimised.

Dettmers et al.\ \cite{dettmers2023qlora} demonstrated that QLoRA can fine-tune a 65-billion-parameter model on a single 48 GB GPU without any degradation in performance relative to a 16-bit full fine-tuning baseline. This result is significant because it democratises access to state-of-the-art LLMs, enabling researchers and practitioners with limited hardware resources to adapt large models to their specific use cases.

The combination of LoRA and QLoRA has become particularly relevant in the context of retrieval-augmented generation systems. Domain-specific RAG pipelines often require the underlying language model to be fine-tuned on the target corpus to improve its ability to process and reason over the retrieved context. Parameter-efficient methods such as LoRA and QLoRA make this adaptation feasible without requiring the substantial computational infrastructure that full fine-tuning would demand.
